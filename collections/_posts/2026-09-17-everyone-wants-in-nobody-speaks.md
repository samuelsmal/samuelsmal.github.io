In my company I participate in and lead a few working groups. There we talk about best practices in
Data Engineering, or Databricks or Agentic Engineering. Usually there is one presenter and a few
people who talk. Usually these groups are formed because two or three people want to discuss a
topic, then discuss it regularly, then others hear about it and feel left out. So the group grows
and grows. At the same time the number of active participants declines: people do not show up, they
just want to be part of the group; or they never say anything except at the end, when they thank
everybody for the productive meeting but have to drop due to an important client meeting.
Participating became a status symbol, something to tell the manager about.

As a result, the output of the group declines. The quality of the discussion decreases. Fewer
decisions are made. Fewer people contribute to the discussion. The result is a lecture-style format
with no non-obvious feedback at the end, whereas at the beginning we had actual pushback on ideas.
And thus it turned into yet another meeting with questionable raison d'être.
At that stage the group is less attractive. Nobody wants to join the group and attendance decreases.
In the sessions fewer and fewer people have the courage to say something, so it evolves into a
monologue of a select few, which is not that interesting after a couple of sessions. And the people
that have something interesting to say will not join, because they are usually also the people that
are in fact busy. Attending another meeting to accumulate tracked time is not in their interest.
They then find another vehicle to discuss their ideas and topics.

The next phase is its slow and painful death.

The unrestrained growth of the group led to an oligopolistic discussion round and then killed the
group itself.

For a long time, I couldn't really explain this without referring to *group dynamics*, or some other
vague term. But then I read an
[article](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/4ZvJab25tDebB8FGE/you-get-about-five-words) on LessWrong.
And the term I was looking for is *bandwidth*, in a double sense: speaking / listening and in-group
capacity per person to contribute.

Which prompted me to read a bit into the subject. While most of the comments of the post (or those I
have read) treat communication as one-directional — how to say something to others — I got drawn
into its bi-directionality. Communication is a two-way street for me (neither surprising nor
insightful, I know). The idea of how I can convey the most information is interesting, but a
different subject; for that, read the comments. Instead, let's talk about:

## How large should interest groups be?

The classical / management question would be: *How large should interest groups be in order to
maximise their efficiency at extracting and disseminating interesting findings?*

The answer is: probably five, maybe seven.

As Fay et al. put it in their 2000 paper [Group Discussion as Interactive Dialogue or as Serial
Monologue: The Influence of Group
Size](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Group-Discussion-as-Interactive-Dialogue-or-as-The-Fay-Garrod/ecf86691b5cbc47362703992d6b664c7bfa4325c):

> The experiments reported here show that in small, 5-person groups, the communication is like
> dialogue and members are influenced most by those with whom they interact in the discussion.
> However, in large, 10-person groups, the communication is like monologue and members are
> influenced most by the dominant speaker.

There is also the **Rule of Seven** from Blenko et al. in their [Decide &
Deliver](https://www.bain.com/insights/books/decide-and-deliver/) book:

> Follow the Rule of Seven for meetings. Who should come to a meeting is always a sensitive issue,
> and the basic precept is often “The more the merrier.” But more is rarely better when it comes to
> making decisions. Our research highlights what we might call the Rule of Seven: every person added
> to a decision-making group over seven reduces decision effectiveness by 10 percent. If you take
> this rule to its logical conclusion, a group of seventeen or more rarely makes any decisions. Of
> course, a larger group may sometimes be necessary to ensure buy-in. But organizations trying to
> make important decisions should limit the size of group as much as possible.

Both are close, but neither is what I want. In groups I don't just want efficiency, I also want to
interact with my colleagues. It's the exchange, the challenging of ideas, the feedback that makes
these groups worthwhile. Bandwidth — the bigger the audience, the fewer words each person gets —
applies directly here.

Another factor: my groups are online. We don't need to stand in a circle, we are not constrained by
the physical realities of the real world, given a decent microphone we can hear each other well.
But that only works if only one person speaks at a time. We could introduce breakout sessions, but
that would defeat the purpose, which is to have a regular chat about a specific topic without much
overhead.

In addition to the sources above, Dunbar et al. argue, in their paper [Size and
structure of freely forming conversational
groups](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02734136), that the limit of individuals who can
interact in spontaneous conversation is about four. [Hackman and
Vidmar](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2786271) arrive at an optimal group size of 4.6, according to
the rating of the members themselves. Hasmi et al.
[claim](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167923622001853) that 25 is the
optimum — but they also look at chatrooms and shared editing, which does not really apply here.
Note that these results are debatable: sample sizes are usually small and outcomes hard to quantify.
However, they all lead towards the same conclusion: One needs to limit the number of participants to
have a fruitful and meaningful and lively discussion. They all agree that the larger the
group, the less effective it becomes. More organisation, more tools are required to steer the
conversation. And in these working groups I don't want to steer too much, the ideas should be
free-flowing, not dominated by one or a few speakers.

The type of dialogue I am looking for is both: a chat and a decision-making forum. A working group
that never decides what the next steps are, or whether there are any follow-ups at all, is just time
we spend together — and that is hard to justify, especially in consulting. In my field, where we
work by billable hours, the loss of revenue is clearly visible, but I would argue the idea applies
to everyone. We should have fun, but the fun needs to have a meaning, otherwise it's a break. And
having a break with 15 people is not my type of break — it feels like work.

Now I need to find a way to kick people out of a group. Or, more realistically, separate
participation from membership, by restructuring the meetings around a core team that debates and
updates the other members in a written post. That will be a tough sell. I will update this post on
how it goes.
